## Overview

A screw is a threaded fastener that cuts or taps its own mating thread as it is driven into a material, or turns into a pre-tapped hole, to hold two parts together. Unlike a bolt, which pairs with a nut (see the Bolts and Nuts pages), most screws grip the base material directly. On electrical orders they fasten boxes, enclosures, panels, strut, straps, and equipment to wood, metal, drywall, and concrete, so one page has to cover several screw families that share a name but not much else. Lag screws are covered on the Bolts page as lag bolts, and the screws built into the rod hangers are on the Threaded Rod Hangers page.

![](/images/screw-example.png)

Screw types:

- **Wood screw** — coarse threads that grip wood, usually with a Phillips drive. Hardwood versions have tighter threads to reduce splitting, and a pilot hole may be needed.
- **Sheet metal screw** — a self-tapping screw for joining thin metal, and also plastic, wood, and aluminum. Sizes are coded by diameter number from 0 to 24, with 6 to 14 the most common.
- **Self-drilling screw (Tek)** — a screw with a drill point that makes its own pilot hole, so no pre-drilling is needed. Common in metal stud framing, light steel framing, and HVAC ductwork. The drill point number (#1 to #5) sets the thickest steel it will drill: #1 up to 20 gauge (0.036"), #2 up to 14 gauge (0.075"), #3 up to 10 gauge (0.135"), #4 up to 1/4" plate, and #5 up to 1/2". Drive at about 1,500 to 2,500 RPM, and note that a standard carbon-steel self-drilling screw will not penetrate stainless steel.
- **Machine screw** — a screw for pre-tapped holes, secured with a nut, used where precision matters, such as equipment, electronics, and appliances.
- **Drywall screw** — a bugle-head screw with a sharp point for fastening drywall. Coarse threads suit wood studs and fine threads suit metal studs.
- **Concrete screw** — a hardened screw with high-low threads for concrete, brick, and block. It needs a pre-drilled hole made with a carbide bit, and it comes with a hex or flat head. Tapcon is the common brand, and the name is often used for the whole family.

Screw features that the order has to name:

- **Head** — Flat, Pan, Round, or Truss for general shapes; Hex-Washer for structural, roofing, and concrete work; Bugle for flush seating in drywall; Wafer for a large bearing surface on cladding and insulation panels.
- **Drive** — Phillips is the most common; Hex (driven with a socket) is used on hex and hex-washer heads; Torx and pin-in-Torx or pin-in-hex are used where a tamper-resistant fastener is wanted.
- **Thread** — coarse for wood and soft materials, fine for metal, and high-low for concrete screws.
- **Finish** — zinc plated, stainless steel, or the blue Climaseal coating that is standard on concrete screws.

Screws are selected based on:

1. **Base material** — wood, thin metal, steel framing or plate, drywall, or concrete and masonry, which sets the type.
2. **Size** — the diameter, as a number (#8, #10, #12) or a fraction of an inch (3/16", 1/4").
3. **Length** — the screw length, chosen for the thickness of the fastened part plus the penetration the base material needs.
4. **Head and drive** — matched to the fixture hole, the bearing surface needed, and the driver on hand.
5. **Finish** — zinc plated for dry indoor use, stainless steel for corrosion resistance, blue Climaseal for concrete screws.

## Naming Convention

When identifying screws for vendor ordering, use the following naming structure, listing attributes in this order:

```
SIZE LENGTH TYPE HEAD DRIVE FINISH Screw
```

### Example Names

- `#8 1-1/2" Wood Flat Phillips Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `#10 1" Sheet-Metal Pan Phillips Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `#10 3/4" Self-Drilling-3 Hex-Washer Hex Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `#12 1" Self-Drilling-2 Pan Phillips Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `#10-32 1" Machine Pan Phillips Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `#6 1-1/4" Drywall Bugle Phillips Zinc-Plated Screw`
- `3/16" 2-3/4" Concrete Flat Phillips Blue-Climaseal Screw`
- `1/4" 3-1/4" Concrete Hex-Washer Hex Stainless-Steel Screw`

### Convention Notes

|Descriptor|Explanation|
|---|---|
|SIZE|The screw diameter: a number size (#6, #8, #10, #12, #14) or a fraction (3/16", 1/4"). For a machine screw, include the thread count (for example #10-32).|
|LENGTH|The screw length in inches (for example 3/4", 1", 1-1/2"). For concrete screws, choose the length as the thickness of the fastened material plus 1" to 1-3/4" of embedment.|
|TYPE|Wood, Sheet-Metal, Self-Drilling, Machine, Drywall, or Concrete. For Self-Drilling, add the drill point number (Self-Drilling-2, Self-Drilling-3, Self-Drilling-5) because the point sets the steel thickness it will drill.|
|HEAD|Flat, Pan, Round, Truss, Hex-Washer, Bugle, or Wafer.|
|DRIVE|Phillips, Hex, or Torx (including the tamper-resistant pin-in styles); confirm other drives, such as slotted or square, in the catalog.|
|FINISH|Zinc-Plated, Stainless-Steel, or Blue-Climaseal (the standard concrete screw coating); other coatings are made, so confirm them in the catalog.|
|Catalog number|Vendor catalogs (e.g., Tapcon, Grabber, Hillman, Fastenal) also carry their own part numbers; provide both the plain-language name and the catalog number when known, since drill bit size, point style, and load ratings are brand- and model-specific.|

### Ordering Help

- Pick the **type by the base material first**: wood screws for wood, self-drilling screws for steel, concrete screws for concrete and masonry, and so on. A wrong-type screw strips, snaps, or does not hold.
- For **self-drilling screws, match the point number to the steel thickness**; a point that is too small for the steel may not finish drilling. Use stainless self-drilling screws, not carbon steel, when the base is stainless steel.
- For **concrete screws, use the drill bit the manufacturer specifies** (for example a 5/32" bit for 3/16" Tapcon screws and a 3/16" bit for 1/4" screws), and a carbide bit, and confirm the hole depth in the manufacturer's instructions. Embedment is 1" at the minimum and 1-3/4" at the maximum.
- Respect **edge distance and spacing** for concrete screws (for example, 1-7/8" from an edge and 3" between 3/16" screws for full rated tension). Published pull-out values are averages, so use a 4:1 safety factor for working loads and follow the project's engineered design.
- **Grounding connections do not use sheet metal screws**; NEC 250.8 calls for listed connectors, clamps, or machine screws with proper thread engagement, so order machine screws for ground connections.
- Match **finish to the environment**: zinc plated for dry indoor use, stainless steel or the manufacturer's corrosion-resistant coating where it is damp or corrosive, keeping the screw compatible with the metal it fastens to avoid galvanic corrosion.
- Order the **driver bits and sockets** that match the head and drive, since a wrong-size bit strips the head.
